02 · THE PROBLEM

Why we need overload protection

▶ WATCH 00:54

The regulator holds V₀ fixed whatever R₀ is. So if R₀ is pulled very low, the current I₀ = V₀ / R₀ grows without limit, and all of it flows through Q1.

PQ1 = VCE · I₀ = (Vi − V₀) · I₀Vi = 15 V, V₀ = 5 V, so VCE = 10 V

Every extra amp costs Q1 another 10 W. In the lecturer's words, without protection "the series pass element will blow off."

  • Slide R₀ down towards a short and watch Q1 heat up.

03 · SIMPLE CURRENT LIMITING

Sense resistor + Q2

▶ WATCH 02:11

Put a small sense resistor RS in series with Q1's emitter, and connect a second transistor Q2 with its base–emitter directly across it.

VBE2 = (V₀ + I₀RS) − V₀ = I₀RS
Set IOC · RS = 0.6 VIOC = overload current, the most you allow
  • I₀ below IOC: VBE2 < 0.6 V, Q2 is off, nothing changes.
  • At IOC: Q2 cuts in. Its collector current diverts Q1's base drive (teal path), pushing Q1 towards cut-off.
  • Pull harder and the current stays clamped at IOC. V₀ drops instead.

CATCHInto a short, Q1 still passes IOC with almost all of Vi across it. Check PQ1 at R₀ = 0.

04 · FOLDBACK CURRENT LIMITING

One change: a divider on Q2's base

▶ WATCH 05:11

The simple limit still pushes IOC into a short. Foldback aims lower: when the output is shorted, the current should drop below the rated current.

The only change: Q2's base is fed from a divider RA/RB across the node V₀ + I₀RS. Q2's emitter stays at V₀.

Vtap = α(V₀ + I₀RS), α = RB/(RA+RB)
VBE2 = α(V₀ + I₀RS) − V₀
  • Toggle between the two circuits.
  • Short the load (R₀ → 0) in each mode and compare the current and Q1's dissipation in the table.
  • Both modes are set to start limiting near 1.2 A.

05 · STEP BY STEP

Deriving the foldback equation

▶ WATCH 07:31
  1. Tap voltage: α(V₀ + I₀RS), α = RB/(RA+RB), 0 < α < 1
  2. Q2's emitter is at V₀, so
    VBE2 = α(V₀ + I₀RS) − V₀
  3. Rearrange:
    VBE2 = αI₀RS − (1 − α)V₀
  4. Solve for I₀:
    I₀ = VBE2/(αRS) + (1 − α)V₀/(αRS)
  5. Short the output: R₀ = 0, so V₀ = 0
    ISC = VBE2/(αRS)
  6. Substitute back:
    I₀ = ISC + (1 − α)V₀/(αRS)

06 · THE FOLDBACK CURVE

The current folds back

▶ WATCH 10:29

Plot V₀ against I₀. In normal use V₀ is flat up to the knee. Ask for more and the operating point slides down a straight line, back to ISC at V₀ = 0.

Iknee = ISC + (1 − α)V₀/(αRS)
ISC = 0.6 / (αRS)The teal term is positive, so ISC < Iknee. That's the "fold".
  • Move R₀: the operating point is where the load line V = I·R₀ meets the curve.
  • Change α and RS to reshape the curve. Smaller α folds back harder.
  • Dashed teal: a simple limit with the same knee, for comparison.

GOING FURTHERFold back too hard and some loads, such as constant-current loads at start-up, can lock the supply at low voltage (Wikipedia: Current limiting).

07 · THE INPUT SIDE · FIRST-CYCLE SURGE

Switch-on: an empty C is a short

▶ WATCH 12:04

Now the bridge + capacitor front end. At switch-on the capacitor is empty, so it sees a voltage step: dV/dt is huge and C behaves like a short.

The first cycle must deliver the whole ½CV₁² of energy. After that, each cycle only tops up what the load used.

ISM ≈ vG(switch-on) / (2rD + Rw)Only two diodes and the winding resistance limit it (0.5 Ω here)
  • Switch-on happens at a random point of the sine. Near the peak is the worst case.
  • Ignore this non-repetitive surge ISM and the diodes or the capacitor can blow in the first half-cycle.

08 · SURGE LIMITING · TRANSFORMER

Leakage inductance helps

▶ WATCH 20:39

A real transformer isn't ideal. Its equivalent circuit is a T network: primary leakage LσP and secondary leakage LσS in series, with the mutual inductance M and core-loss RC in shunt.

M and RC are large, so the surge ignores them. It flows through both leakage inductances, which act as an impedance in its path.

Z = jω(LσP + LσS) + RXL = 2π·50·Lσ. The current also can't jump instantly.

That's why transformer-fed supplies often need no extra protection. The lecturer still advises adding some, to be safe.

09 · SURGE LIMITING · NTC THERMISTOR

Hot when needed, cool when not

▶ WATCH 22:57

Add a negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistor in series: its resistance falls as it heats up.

  • Cold at switch-on: high R limits the first-cycle surge.
  • Running: Irms²R heats it, R drops, and the steady-state loss becomes small.
  • The lecturer's sketch: 10 Ω cold, about 5 Ω hot.
Datasheet shown in the lecture:

RECAP

What to take away

  1. Protect Q1. A short makes I₀ huge, and PQ1 = VCE·I₀ destroys it.
  2. Simple limit: VBE2 = I₀RS, so the current clamps at IOC = 0.6 V / RS.
  3. Foldback: a divider on Q2's base gives I₀ = ISC + (1 − α)V₀/(αRS). Into a short only ISC flows.
  4. First-cycle surge: an empty C draws a non-repetitive ISM, worst when switched on near the AC peak.
  5. Tame it with leakage inductance and a cold NTC thermistor, whose resistance falls as it warms.
